**Rating: 3/5**

![cover art](https://cdn.thestorygraph.com/x97jrl8paam1coxzthacqtncynzh)

I have mixed feelings about this book.

On one hand it was an interesting read for me since at the moment I'm building my "philosophical knowledge". And so it was interesting reading about Kant, Rousseau, Hume, etc. from the pen of an expert in the matter. Also, the idea of the book is something that resonates with me quite a lot. The author poses questions that I pose to myself everyday, and I was therefore eager for an in depth analysis and some suggestions or general guidelines on how to deal with the hardship of becoming an adult in this day and age.

On the other hand, Neiman (spoiler alert) doesn't know the answer. In the end it seems she's in a similar position herself, and she's trying to cope with it.

The point of the book is that society doesn't want people to become adults, and so in a sort of spontaneous manner (as opposed to a "conspiracy theory" framing), as a natural mechanism of causes and effects, it will make it hard. Society, by its nature, and we're eager for comfort, and so we collude with it. We then have built narratives about how the best part of our lives is childhood, and then maybe it is adolescence, and in general it's "youth". This myth of youth reflects itself also into the consumerist society built by neo-liberism, based on planned obsolescence of products, and the generation of false needs to push economic growth at all costs (helped by the infamous advertising industry). And then by contrast to all this, as a consequence rather, life becomes perceived as a downhill process, a losing game towards the misery and irrelevance of old age. Also, the narrative is reinforced by describing adulthood as a "serious", grim state of affairs. You gotta grow up, leave alone all those silly dreams, all these rebellious ideas, get a job, make a family, and shut up.

There is an "is" and there is an "ought". There is reality as is, and there is a way we think (and feel) reality ought to be. This tension is unsolvable, and accepting to live with this tension is the meaning of becoming an adult.
Accepting the world for the shit that it is, whilst not giving up on our ideals and dreams, and keep pushing for a change, at least in our own little, insignificant life.

I totally embrace this progressive vision of life, it's political, it's a stance that I agree with.

But that's it. There's no more hope than this in this book if you were wondering, and somehow it's a shame, I have a bitter taste in my mouth.

Is that the taste of adulthood?

I'm somewhat looking forward to season 2, but I have a strong feeling that it's been cancelled.
