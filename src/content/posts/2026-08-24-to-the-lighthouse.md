**Rating: 3/5**

![cover art](https://cdn.thestorygraph.com/ng5ieefj3p2e0bageqy2ha24torh)

This was possibly the novel that took me the most time to read per page ever, on par with [Moby-Dick](/posts/2026-06-13-moby-dick) maybe. It was very dense and cognitively demanding.

I would describe it as a novel in 3 acts:

in the first act we are presented with an extremely detailed slice of life from the beginning of the 20th century. The place is a mansion house on the isle of Skye, Scotland. The people are a family of husband, wife, and 8 children, and a group of about 5 guests, friends from the city of London.

We go through this slice of life by inhabiting the consciousness of several characters; the focus changes constantly from one mind to another, pivoting on some external elements that tie all this reality together; a hedge, a window, the sea, the lighthouse in the distance. And so for example we move from the stream of consciousness of the wife, and therefore her point of view, to the one of the husband, passing through their impressions of the view outside the window.

Not much happens at all, but there is a huge depth in the descriptions of the characters' feelings, thoughts, and emotions. Also, the constant change of point of view conveys very well the relativity of the perception of life and reality itself.

Then the second part came in, unexpected:

where the first section built up these very detailed interior worlds, the second comes like a hurricane, undoing it all.

I had to read the whole of the second chapter aloud, it was more akin to poem than prose, at least to me. And it was magnificent. Ten years pass in the few pages, where the main characters are now the house itself, withstanding the elements, and the relentless passing of time.
Very vivid imagery, potent descriptions. I loved it. People here are in the background, and this really gave to me the sense of how small are our lives compared to time and space.

The third and final chapter gets back to the style of the first, whilst dealing with the aftermath of the events of the second chapter.

A nice contrast appears then, great feelings of mourning and sadness, and longing, and questioning the meaning of life.

My biggest gripe with this whole novel is simple: I just couldn't connect in the slightest with these people, a group of "upper class" English "intellectuals" in the early 20th century vacationing in Scotland. I just didn't care about their lives, I even despised them, most of the time, in their privileged and whiny existence. They were so distant from my view of the world, but then again maybe it was also one of the points of the novel? Because there is indeed some generational friction here, but still, very far away from my experience. The exposure of sexism was quite obvious, it goes without saying.

Overall I liked the novel, I'd say. But it was a real chore. My main enjoyment came from the writing style and the narrative mechanism, which were both indeed masterful but again, also very demanding.
